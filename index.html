How video games grew up

From Atari cartridges to Call of Duty, games went from a niche toy to one of the world’s biggest entertainment industries. This story follows 35 years of best-selling games across five “levels”: the console eras, the genres we love, the companies that make them, the markets that buy them, and finally Australia, where nine in ten households now play.

Level 1

The eras of play

The video game market has crashed, been reborn and changed hands several times. Each era was defined by the consoles people bought, and by who made them.

17M → 679M copies sold in 1983, the year of the crash, versus the 2008 peak

After the 1983 crash wiped out Atari, Nintendo rebuilt the market almost alone: in 1990 its platforms carried 95% of sales. Sony’s PlayStation took the lead from 1995, and Microsoft’s Xbox joined in 2001.

The drop after 2008 is partly real, as the Wii and DS craze faded. It is also partly a blind spot: VGChartz counts boxed copies, and more and more games were being downloaded instead.

Level 2

What we play

A single smash hit can define a whole year. Over time, players moved from jumping on platforms to shooting in first person.

In 1985, platform games were 80% of all sales, almost entirely because of Super Mario Bros. By 2015 they were just 2%. Shooters rose from 3% in 2000 to 25% in 2015, and action games became the biggest genre of all.

Action is the biggest genre, with 1.7 billion copies, yet no one owns it: 64% of its sales come from publishers outside its top four. Other genres are near-monopolies. EA sold 458 million sports games, and Nintendo sold more than half of all platformers.

82.7M copies of Wii Sports, the best-selling game in the data. It came bundled with the Wii console in most countries.

Switch between eras to see how each generation’s blockbusters changed, from Duck Hunt and Tetris to Pokémon, Grand Theft Auto and Call of Duty.

Level 3

Who makes the games

Publishers fund, market and sell games. Their league table tells the story of a power shift from Japan to the United States.

Nintendo finished first in four of the six eras and second in the other two. Electronic Arts made the biggest climb, from 30th in 1986–94 to 1st in 2001–05. US publishers like Activision, Take-Two (publisher of Grand Theft Auto) and Warner Bros. rose with the HD consoles, and France’s Ubisoft broke into the top five.

28% of Japanese publishers’ games were sold in Japan. The rest went overseas.

Kyoto (Nintendo alone, 1.78 billion copies) and Tokyo (eight publishers, 1.68 billion) are the two biggest dots on the map. Japanese publishers sold their games mainly abroad, especially to North America.

58% of North American publishers’ games were sold at home.

US publishers relied on their huge home market and sold very little in Japan. European publishers such as Ubisoft sold more in North America (51%) than in Europe (35%).

The rest of the world, Australia included, bought between 8% and 11% of each group’s games. Hover the rings for the exact numbers.

Level 4

Where games are played

VGChartz splits sales into four regions: North America, Europe, Japan and the rest of the world, which includes Australia. Each has its own taste.

Role-playing games such as Pokémon, Final Fantasy and Dragon Quest make up 27% of Japan’s sales, compared with about 8% in every other region. Shooters show the reverse: about 13% of sales in North America, Europe and the rest of the world, but only 3% in Japan.

Australia sits in the “rest of the world” region, whose tastes closely match Europe’s and North America’s. Action and sports games lead.

40% → 12% Japan’s share of world game sales, 1986–94 versus 2013–15

Europe grew from 6% to 36% of sales, and the rest of the world, Australia included, from about 1% to 11%.

Level 5

Australia: player two has entered

Australia was always a small slice of world sales, but it is now a nation of players, and a growing exporter of games.

90% of Australian households play video games

Gaming is no longer a teenage hobby. If you gathered 100 Australians who play video games, 71 would be of working age, 19 would be children and 10 would be retirees. The average player is about 35.

And for the first time, more women (51%) than men (48%) play.

Victoria has 30% of the industry’s workers and 27% of its studios. Queensland and New South Wales follow, and together the three east-coast states employ 80% of game developers.

The two measures tell different stories. Comparing them (below) shows where the big studios are.

When a state’s share of workers is bigger than its share of studios, its studios are bigger than average. Queensland has 17% of studios but 27% of workers.

Western Australia is the reverse: 14% of studios but only 8% of workers, a scene made up of small independent teams.

$115M → $339M revenue of Australian game studios, 2015–16 to 2023–24

Workers grew almost as fast, from 842 to about 2,450, and 95% of the money comes from overseas. Australian games are made for the same global market charted above.

The jump to $608.5M in 2024–25 is partly a change of method. IGEA added sales data from the analytics firm Sensor Tower for the first time, so that bar is not directly comparable with earlier years.

Game over?

Four things to remember

  • Eras are made by consolesNintendo, then Sony, then a three-way race with Microsoft. Each new console generation reshaped what sold.
  • Hits define genresSuper Mario Bros., Pokémon, Wii Sports and Call of Duty each pushed one genre to the top of its era.
  • Power moved westJapan went from making all six of the top publishers to just one, and from buying 40% of games to 12%.
  • Australia is levelling upNine in ten households play, and local studios now earn hundreds of millions of dollars, almost all of it from overseas.
